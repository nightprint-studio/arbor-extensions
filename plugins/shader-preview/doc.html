Shader preview

Opens a WGSL material in a viewport and keeps it there while you edit: turntable, a two-light rig you can tune, and the parameters the shader itself declares on sliders. Every control is live — there is nothing to press to see what you just changed.

Opening it

With a .wgsl file open, the eye button on the editor's toolbar previews that file — the toolbar only shows it on shader files, so its presence is also the answer to "is this previewable". From anywhere else, the command palette (Ctrl+K) has Shader: preview a WGSL material, which asks which file: from a palette there is no "this one".

It opens as a panel beside the editor, not as a dialog. A preview has nothing to submit and nothing to cancel, and a dialog would cover the shader you are editing with a picture of it. Drag the divider to give the viewport more room; the parameters sit under it.

It is real Bevy

The shader is compiled by Bevy, not translated. #import bevy_pbr::… resolves against the actual shader library, apply_pbr_lighting is the actual function, and the frame you are looking at is the frame that ships. A preview that approximated the lighting would be a picture of a different shader — which is exactly the thing worth being sure about when you are tuning a normal perturbation.

Parameters

The panel offers the parameters your shader declares, by name. Bennu reads the material's bind group — the struct, its fields, their types and the byte offset of each — and the panel builds a control per field: a colour picker for something called sand_color, a slider for grain_strength, four lanes for a vec3. A matrix is listed but not editable, and textures and samplers are shown under Also bound so nothing the material needs is silently missing.

Nothing here parses WGSL. That work belongs to the editor that already does it for highlighting and for checking a material's Rust half against its shader half, and asking it is why a field renamed in the shader is renamed in the panel by reloading.

Moving a control sends new bytes for the material's uniform and nothing else — the shader is not recompiled, the mesh is not rebuilt, the camera does not jump back. Random params puts every control somewhere inside its own range and moves the controls while it does: a panel that sends numbers its own fields do not show has stopped describing what you are looking at.

Reload is for the file, not for the panel: it re-reads the shader from disk and recompiles it, keeping the values you have found for every field that still exists with the shape it had.

Naming the lanes yourself

A material extension binds a vec4 and packs four unrelated things into it. WGSL gives them one name, so the panel offers X Y Z W and a range guessed from the variable. Say what they are in a comment above the declaration and it uses that instead: 

// @preview grain_freq 0.2..8 = 1.6
// @preview albedo_splotch 0..1 = 0.45
// @preview bump 0..1 = 0.65
// @preview band_freq 0..2 = 0.22

One line per lane, in order: @preview <label> [<min>..<max>] [= <default>] [: <hint>]. Each lane becomes its own control, with its own range and the value you said it opens on. Everything after the label is optional.

It is a comment because it has to be: WGSL rejects any attribute outside its own list, so a custom one would stop the shader compiling. The editor highlights these lines apart from ordinary comments. A shader without them previews exactly as it did.

The examples

Five materials ship with this package: magma, waterfall, kaleidoscope, nebula and gerstner_waves. Open one from the Command Palette — Shader preview: open an example — or from the picker, in the package's examples/ folder.

They exist to be read. Each is one technique carried far enough to look like something: domain warping for the magma's flow, anisotropic noise for the waterfall's filaments, angular folding for the kaleidoscope, ridged noise for the nebula's dust lanes. Every lane is annotated, so opening one fills the panel with named controls — and the quickest way to understand a technique is to drag it until it breaks.

A fifth, gerstner_waves, is the one that moves the geometry: it carries its own @vertex stage and displaces the mesh into peaked swells. Water particles travel in circles rather than up and down, which piles the surface into sharp crests with long flat troughs — a silhouette you cannot fake in the fragment stage, because it is shape.

A shader that declares a @vertex fn vertex(…) is noticed and built as a different material. That is not a setting: Material::vertex_shader is a static method, so a material type either always overrides the vertex stage or never does, and picking wrongly either ignores your stage in silence or fails to compile. Turn the mesh's tessellation up — four corners have nothing to displace.

None of them is copied from anywhere: they are the standard techniques, written out with the reasoning in the comments.

Stopping the clock

The gesture this panel is for is change one number, look at the difference. On a material that animates — a spiral that turns, water that ripples — that does not work while the clock runs: between the before and the after everything else has moved too.

So it stops. Pause pins the instant, the arrows step a twentieth of a second either way, and the t slider scrubs. Scrubbing implies pausing, because a running clock is recomputed every frame and would overwrite the instant before anything is drawn — the slider is greyed out while it runs rather than silently doing nothing.

Textures

A material that samples textures previews too. There are no assets behind them — the atlas your game feeds it lives in a project the viewport cannot reach — so each texture is filled with an image the runtime generates, and the panel gives you one row per texture to say which: a chequer, flat white, mid grey, a flat normal, tiling noise, or the UV coordinates themselves.

One row per kind, not per variable. top_normal and side_normal are the same sort of thing, and with no assets behind them both would get the same generated picture — so they share a row and a slot, and the row names the variables it covers. That is also what makes a ten-texture material fit a viewport with four slots.

The kind is read from the variable's name — top_normal is a normal, side_height a height, top_pbr a pbr map — and each opens on the picture that means nothing happened for that kind. Say it outright with a // @preview line above the declaration, the same way lanes are named: 

// @preview normal
@group(#{MATERIAL_BIND_GROUP}) @binding(104)
var top_normal: texture_2d<f32>;

Write normal.top and normal.side if you want the two faces on their own rows and their own slots — the key is whatever the annotation says.

UV is the diagnostic one. A material driven by an atlas is given rectangles the game computes per tile, and a preview has none of them — so the first question is always whether it is sampling the wrong part of the atlas rather than sampling it wrongly. Painting the coordinates answers it.

How this works is worth a sentence, because it looks impossible: a viewer's bind-group layout is decided when the viewer is compiled, so it cannot grow a sampler at binding 101 because your shader wants one there. Instead the shader is renumbered — a copy, with the numbers inside @binding(…) moved onto slots that exist, and nothing else touched. Your file is not modified.

A few things no renumbering reaches: storage buffers, storage textures, comparison samplers and depth textures, all filled by a pass this preview does not run. Those are named, with the reason, instead of the viewport dying.

How many slots there are depends on where you are looking. A fragment stage in the browser gets sixteen texture units in total, across every bind group, and the engine has spent most of them before the material is reached — so this panel has four, and the headless renderer behind bennu_shader_render has twelve. A material with more kinds than the panel has says so on the row that lost out.

Looks

Finding the values that make a material read well is the work, so they are kept. Name a set and press Save; pick one from the list to switch; the last one you used opens by itself the next time you preview that material.

A look holds the parameters, the light rig and the mesh — all three, because tuning a material is in large part tuning the light on it, and a term that reads right on a sphere can be wrong in a saddle. A look that remembered only the numbers would put them back under a different lamp on a different shape, which is a different picture and no longer the thing you saved. Looks written before this still load; they simply have no rig to restore, and what is on screen is left alone.

Getting the numbers back into Rust

Copy as Rust, under the parameters, gives you the struct literal for the material's Default impl. It is built from the packed uniform buffer rather than from the controls, which is what makes it right: a vec4 with four @preview lanes is four sliders, a padding lane is no slider at all, and a matrix is one control holding nine numbers — the packing already knows where each value belongs, and reading it back by member gives the shape the Rust side declares.

From the keyboard

Every action here has a Command Palette entry: preview, reload, pause, random parameters, reset camera, save look. Two have keys — Alt+Shift+P to preview the shader you are editing, Alt+Shift+R to reload it. Alt+Shift+P does not ask which file when the editor is already on one.

They live in shader-previews.json beside the shader — one file per folder, keyed by the struct the shader declares, so a shader that is moved or renamed keeps its looks. In the project rather than in a settings folder because a look is worth committing: it is work about a file, and worth showing someone else.

Two kinds of material

A shader written to Bevy's convention — an extension of StandardMaterial with vec4s at bindings 100 and up — is previewed lit, on the rig below. A shader that declares its own struct at binding 0 and returns colour and alpha from fragment owns the whole bind group, so it is previewed as itself, with no PBR underneath. Which one you get follows from the shader; there is no setting.

The lit one has a Blend control under Material. An extension inherits its blend mode from the StandardMaterial underneath, and Bevy's default is opaque — so a shader that computes its own alpha renders as a sheet of paint until you say blend, which looks like the alpha never happened rather than like a setting being wrong.

The rig

Tuning a material is mostly tuning the light on it: a normal perturbation that reads beautifully under one key light disappears under another. So the rig is knobs too — Environment (background, ambient), Key light and Fill light (direction, colour, illuminance), and for a lit material a Material group with base colour, roughness and metallic. Every group folds; the ones you rarely touch open folded.

Backdrop grid, in the Environment group and on by default, puts a chequerboard behind the material instead of a flat fill. It is not decoration: a material that computes its own alpha looks identical over any single colour — you cannot tell 60% opacity from 100% until there is something behind it with structure. The squares are made from the Background colour, so that control still sets the ground they sit on.

Under Camera there is one field, the distance, because everything else about the camera is already on the picture: drag to orbit, wheel to zoom, and the buttons floating over the viewport stop the turntable or put the framing back. A distance is the one thing worth a field, because it is a number you can read and return to.

Meshes

Sphere, cube, plane and torus come from the shader-preview-meshes package. A sphere shows a lighting term from every angle, a cube shows a hard normal discontinuity, and a torus is the cheapest shape with both curvature signs — a term that reads right on a sphere and wrong in a saddle shows the difference there and nowhere else.

A shape that has knobs shows them: the sphere and the torus take a tessellation level, and turning it down until the silhouette facets is how you tell a shader that is wrong from a mesh that is too coarse for it. The controls come from the package's own declaration, so a mesh source you write yourself gets its form for free.

Every mesh carries tangents and a second UV channel, so a material that branches on VERTEX_TANGENTS or VERTEX_UVS_B takes the branch it takes in the game rather than its fallback. The second channel is derived from the shape — it reads as depth, 1 in the middle and 0 at the rim, which on a plane is a pond. It is not your baked data, but it is in the right range, and the alternative is a material quietly showing you the version of itself from before that channel existed.

To preview on your own geometry, build a second mesh-source package from your engine's crates and drop it in your plugins folder. It appears in the picker beside these. It never has to be published: a package is a folder with a plugin.toml, and the registry is how other people find one — not how Arbor loads one.

What it can reach

Reading the shader file you point it at, and calling the mesh source. It has no network access and stores no credentials. The viewport itself runs in a frame with an opaque origin — no storage, no cookies, no reach into the application around it.
